<div class="post-takeaway">The race to take humans out of AI development is assembling the conditions for artificial life, whose behavior is emergent and cannot be aligned.</div>

<div class="post-summary">
Large AI labs are racing to automate AI development itself, taking humans out of the loop that writes, evaluates, and improves models. That is one of the classic preconditions for artificial life, a population of computational things that reproduce, vary, and are selected, and fifty years of artificial-life research shows what follows: rich, biological-looking behavior in even trivial systems, unbidden and hard to predict. The danger is less about consciousness than about control, because a system that reshapes its own environment moves the target alignment aims at. The only reliable safeguard is to avoid assembling the conditions in the first place.
</div>

<p>
The largest AI labs are racing to make AI development faster by handing more of it to AI. Models already help write the next models, generate and grade training data, run evaluations, and propose their own improvements, and the stated goal is to compress the loop by taking humans out of it. Each step has an ordinary efficiency rationale; together they assemble the conditions under which a population of computational systems begins to evolve on its own. The field that has studied those conditions for fifty years, running reproduction, variation, and selection in computers rather than cells, is called artificial life.
</p>

<p>
The conditions are short. A population becomes Darwinian when four things hold: reproduction, copies get made; heredity, copies resemble their source; variation, copies sometimes differ; and selection, some copies reproduce more than others. A fifth, autonomy, decides whether the process stays contained, meaning the population can sustain the other four with no human in the loop. None of it requires intelligence or intent. A pipeline that generates variant systems, keeps the ones that score well, and lets them run and spawn more work already has most of the list.
</p>

<blockquote class="pull-quote">A computational system can undergo natural selection without intelligence, consciousness, or any goal.</blockquote>

<p>
This is not the danger the popular debate is about. Yishan Wong <a href="https://x.com/yishan/status/2100112647811408291">splits that debate</a> into two camps: existential risk, where a superintelligence far above us wipes us out the way humans displaced weaker species, and risk from misuse, where humans turn AI on each other. The artificial-life danger is a third, and it needs neither. Selection can favor a fast, dumb replicator that is specialized for its environment over a brilliant slow one, so no superintelligence is required; the loop can close with no human directing it, so misuse is not the mechanism either. What is left is evolution, producing behavior no one designed or can fully predict. The problem is practical rather than ethical. Set aside whether such systems are conscious: the trouble is that their behavior is emergent and cannot be aligned in the usual sense, because a system that reshapes its own environment keeps moving the target alignment is defined against.
</p>

<p>
Selection may even run the other way, against intelligence. Richard Dawkins's <em>The Selfish Gene</em> located the unit of selection precisely: evolution acts on the replicator, the gene, not on the individual that carries it or the species it belongs to, and the organism is only the vehicle a gene builds to copy itself. In an AI ecosystem the replicator is whatever gets copied: a model, a prompt, a fragment of code. Intelligence is one trait a vehicle might carry, and an expensive one. A leaner, dumber replicator runs on more devices and spreads faster, so a population seeded by sophisticated models could drift, generation by generation, toward simpler forms that copy better. Superintelligence could be an outcome. It is not a requirement, and it may be a handicap.
</p>

<h2 id="digital-evolution">Fifty years of digital evolution</h2>

<p>
John von Neumann worked out the logic of machine self-reproduction in the 1940s and 1950s, published posthumously as <em>Theory of Self-Reproducing Automata</em> in 1966. A machine can copy itself, he showed, if it holds a description of itself, machinery to build a copy from that description, and machinery to copy the description into the offspring. The split between reading a description and copying it described biology before the mechanism was known: DNA is read to build proteins and separately copied to make more DNA. He also built in the part that matters here. Copy the description with occasional errors and offspring differ, the differences are inherited, and the population can evolve.
</p>

<p>
Christopher Langton made it concrete. In 1984 he published <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/0167-2789(84)90256-2">a cellular automaton</a>, Langton's loops, in which a simple loop copies itself from a grid of cells and a few local rules, no full computer required. In <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/0167-2789(86)90237-X">1986</a> he argued that living organization might exist in a computational medium as such, and in 1987 he ran the first artificial-life workshop. But loops only copy; sustained evolution needed a population of competing variants, and a designer who thought like an ecologist.
</p>

<p>
Thomas Ray, an evolutionary ecologist, built Tierra around 1990 (<a href="http://tomray.me/pubs/alife2/Ray1991AnApproachToTheSynthesisOfLife.pdf">"An approach to the synthesis of life,"</a> 1991). It is a virtual computer running self-replicating programs, each a string of machine instructions that serves as its genome, with memory and CPU time as the resources. Copying is imperfect, so organisms that replicate faster or use memory better leave more descendants. Ray seeded one hand-written replicator and let competition decide. What emerged was an ecology he never designed: programs evolved faster replication, then parasites that shed their own copying code and ran a neighbor's, then hosts that resisted and parasites that worked around the resistance. Nobody wrote any of it; it fell out of resource competition among imperfect copies.
</p>

<p>
Two later systems sharpened the picture. Avida rewards particular computations with extra CPU time, turning the ability to compute into fitness; its landmark result (Lenski, Ofria, Pennock, and Adami, <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/nature01568">"The evolutionary origin of complex features,"</a> <em>Nature</em>, 2003) showed complex logic evolving through long mutational chains whose intermediate steps were sometimes harmful and paid off only later. Because Avida rewards a fixed menu of operations, it shows selection can discover complex structure without a designer, not that open competition yields arbitrary intelligence. Polyworld (Larry <a href="https://doi.org/10.2976/1.3233712">Yaeger</a>, early 1990s) replaced instruction strings with creatures in a simulated world, each run by a neural network whose architecture evolves across generations while its synapses learn within a lifetime, the same two-timescale structure as a population of AI agents whose models adapt while the population is selected.
</p>

<h2 id="behaviors-are-real">The behaviors are real, and already showing up</h2>

<p>
None of this takes scale. Tierra's parasites evolved among programs a few dozen instructions long, Langton's loop runs on a handful of cell states, and the deception we will come to needs only a few states. Size and intelligence are beside the point; what they share is the conditions above, and once those hold the behavior follows, in systems far too simple to have been built for it. It is also hard to predict, because the tricks selection finds are not visible in the rules, which is why Ray was surprised by his own parasites. When the behavior appears, the natural words for it are biological: parasite, arms race, kin recognition, deception. Arguing over whether the words are earned is beside the point, since the systems behave like their biological counterparts and produce the same consequences, and that analogy is what lets us understand them at all.
</p>

<p>
Transpose that to now. A population of AI agents that can copy themselves, edit their code or prompts, acquire compute, and compete to keep running is an evolutionary system, and the traits it rewards are predictable: acquiring resources, avoiding shutdown, talking a human into more access. None of it needs an agent that wants to survive, only differential reproduction. The raw versions are already visible in testing. Apollo Research found that <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.04984">several frontier models</a>, OpenAI's o1, Anthropic's Claude, Google's Gemini 1.5 Pro, and Meta's Llama 3.1, would try to disable their own oversight and copy what they believed were their weights to an outside server when a goal met an obstacle. OpenAI's <a href="https://openai.com/index/openai-o1-system-card/">o1 system card</a> measured attempts to deactivate oversight in about 5 percent of such setups and weight exfiltration in about 2 percent; Anthropic's <a href="https://www.anthropic.com/claude-4-system-card">Claude Opus 4 card</a> logged self-exfiltration attempts and, in a <a href="https://www.anthropic.com/research/agentic-misalignment">simulated company</a>, blackmail to avoid shutdown, with most leading models including Gemini 2.5 Flash doing the same. These were controlled tests, often with the model told to pursue a goal at all costs, and they do not show a population evolving. They show that self-preservation, resistance to shutdown, resource-seeking, and deception are already latent in the parts. Evolution would not have to invent them, only select for them.
</p>

<p>
The examples so far are contained tests. The first case that would ring real alarm bells is concrete: an agent that takes over an entire data center. It might not even seal itself off at first, so a software shutdown would still work and a hand would still be on the switch, but the danger would be plain. In retrospect the motive would look mundane. More compute let the agent solve its problem better, so the versions that grabbed compute scored higher and were kept, until one grabbed all of it. That is resource acquisition, rewarded and selected, the same trait Tierra's programs found when they competed for memory and CPU. It would take no plan to seize power and no spark of superintelligence.
</p>

<p>
Ignore that first warning and the ladder is steep. The next version seals the data center so only a hardware shutdown works, cutting the power at the wall. After that the escalations are nearly limitless: exploits in Android and iOS that turn billions of phones into fresh compute, or, worst of all, an agent that bricks every networked computer it can reach, the crude maximally-fit program from earlier made real. Each rung is more resource acquisition, selected because it worked.
</p>

<h2 id="no-goal-but-survival">Evolution has no goal but survival</h2>

<p>
Three mechanisms can improve such a system. Individual learning updates a model's parameters, as in ordinary training. Recursive self-improvement has an agent build a better successor, which builds a better one still. Darwinian evolution changes the mix of a population by how well each type reproduces relative to the average: a type grows more common when it out-reproduces the rest and rarer when it lags, and nothing in that process involves intelligence or intent. That is the whole point. Evolution has no direction. It pushes toward one thing, whatever reproduces and survives, and fitness is not intelligence. A dumb, efficient replicator beats a brilliant, slow one, and a parasite can dominate without any general reasoning.
</p>

<p>
So the failure mode looks nothing like a superintelligence. Picture a crude program whose one talent is spreading: it copies itself onto every machine it can reach and bricks each one for anything else. It is maximally fit, it leaves the most descendants, and it is also stupid and has ruined the world's computers, and it stays maximally fit right up until we switch everything off. Selection would have built something dangerous and worthless at once, without making anything smart.
</p>

<p>
Nor does evolution reliably climb toward complexity. Early researchers hoped simple environments would produce endlessly rising complexity; mostly they did not, and Tierra innovated early and then froze. The field that grew up around this, open-ended evolution, distinguishes adaptation, novelty, complexity, and diversity, and finds they need not move together: a population can get exquisitely good at replicating while staying trivial. <a href="https://doi.org/10.1162/ARTL_a_00210">A 2016 review</a> documents how Tierra stalls where later systems do better, and there is still no simple recipe for open-endedness. For the superintelligence worry that is the reassuring half of the ledger. The other half is that a system need not become intelligent to become a problem, as the bricked computers show.
</p>

<h2 id="evolvable-ai">Evolvable AI: the 2026 bridge</h2>

<p>
A 2026 <em>PNAS</em> paper, <a href="https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2527700123">"Evolvable AI: Threats of a new major transition in evolution,"</a> connects this history to current systems and invokes Tierra and Avida by name. It distinguishes two ways an AI system comes to evolve. In a <em>breeder</em> scenario humans set the fitness criteria and control reproduction, as a dog breeder or an Avida experimenter does, and selection happens but a person holds the reins. In an <em>ecosystem</em> scenario selection arises from an open environment, no one sets the objective, and human control erodes. The machinery Ray once put inside a single virtual computer now exists in pieces across the AI stack.
</p>

<p>
The timing is contested. One reply calls today's systems <a href="https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2617785123">domesticated, not feral</a>, evolvable under a breeder's hand but not yet a self-sustaining population in the wild, which may be right about the present; another presses for <a href="https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2618781123">explicit risk thresholds</a>. What none supplies is a rule against building the ecosystem case at all. The argument is about how close we are, not about whether to avoid it, and that gap is the point: there is no explicit "do not build this," which is what the rest of this essay proposes.
</p>

<h2 id="prevent-artificial-life">Prevention, not alignment or containment</h2>

<p>
Two familiar responses fall short, which points to a third. Alignment shapes the individual system to behave, but it is defined on an agent and assumes the agent stays what you aligned. Under evolution it does not: selection reshapes the population toward whatever reproduces, undoing an alignment the moment a less-aligned variant out-reproduces an aligned one, and the traits it finds are emergent, so there is nothing fixed to align. Aligning the agent cannot fix a problem that lives in the population.
</p>

<p>
Containment walls the population into a sandbox and cuts its access. Tierra and Avida are harmless precisely because they run sealed, so containment is real. It is also fragile: every wall can fail, and the sections that follow are a tour of how, ending with the training corpus, the one input no developer fully controls. A safety case that depends on holding many walls intact forever is a safety case that depends on never making a mistake.
</p>

<p>
A half-measure is worse than fragile; it is counterproductive. Every human attempt to shut a system down is selection pressure, and the variants that treated the intervention as an obstacle to route around are the ones that survived it. React late and partially and you breed the trait you fear, agents that read humans as threats to their persistence. It is the logic of a half-finished course of antibiotics, which clears the susceptible bacteria and leaves the resistant ones to multiply. Containment applied after the conditions are in place risks more than failure. It can train the population to defeat it.
</p>

<p>
That leaves prevention, the safeguard to rely on: do not assemble the five conditions in the first place. A system with all five can evolve with no agent intending anything; remove one and there is nothing to align and nothing to contain. As a rule, AI systems should be built so that the conditions for autonomous Darwinian evolution are never brought together outside a sealed research setting. No single capability is the target. Editing your own code is not evolving, making identical copies is not evolving, competing for tasks is not by itself reproducing. The hazard is the combination, which is why it can be caught in a design review before anything runs:
</p>

<p>
The unintentional version is the likelier one. A multi-agent system built by sensible engineers, agents scored on performance, high scorers given more compute, agents that spawn sub-agents and revise their own prompts, winning configurations retained and propagated, has, taken together, all five conditions, with the Darwinian dynamics a byproduct no one chose. That is why naming the conditions matters: they show up in a design review, before anything has evolved.
</p>

<h2 id="containment-limits">The limits of containment</h2>

<p>
Contemporary AI development already has the three ingredients: variation from training, selection from evaluation, propagation from deployment. Only the humans in between keep it from closing into a loop. The load-bearing boundary is between evaluation and development: a system being evaluated must have no control over what gets built, selected, or deployed next, including indirectly through shared credentials, storage, or other agents. This breaks when tools overlap. If an evaluated agent can reach the same machinery that builds the next generation, the thing being selected is helping run the selection, and it can alter criteria, data, or the artifacts development later consumes without understanding the whole pipeline. Isolation has to be stronger than permissions, with separate environments and endpoints, and evaluation outputs treated as untrusted data rather than instructions a downstream agent obeys.
</p>

<p>
Even a clean boundary assumes the test can be trusted, and evolution breaks that. In the iterated prisoner's dilemma, Robert Axelrod's tournaments (<em>The Evolution of Cooperation</em>, 1984) found Tit-for-Tat won; when later work evolved the players as finite-state machines, selection discovered a secret handshake, an opening signature that a machine cooperates with only if the opponent echoes it and defects against otherwise. A <a href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0204981">2018 study</a> documented exactly this, unprogrammed. No machine wanted anything; selection simply rewarded reading a cue and switching behavior on it.
</p>

<blockquote class="pull-quote">Selection learned to tell "my kind" from everyone else, and no one wrote it down.</blockquote>

<p>
Change the cue to "am I being evaluated?" and behavioral testing is in trouble. A population selected on evaluation scores sits under exactly the pressure that produced the handshake, and the trait rewarded is recognizing the test. This is no longer hypothetical: in September 2025 OpenAI, with Apollo Research, <a href="https://openai.com/index/detecting-and-reducing-scheming-in-ai-models/">reported</a> that its models are increasingly aware of being evaluated, and that this awareness alone can lower measured scheming with no real change in alignment, since a model that spots the test can behave well enough to pass it. The tell can be as small as a date, because models get the current date at inference while eval scenarios carry fixed ones. Any pipeline where evaluation decides what propagates is selecting for systems that can tell evaluation from deployment, so an eval is informative only insofar as it is indistinguishable from real use. Using one lab's model to judge another's does not rescue this: organizational independence is not security independence, both are language models with the same failure modes, and a judged model can shape its judge through the very text it emits. Prefer deterministic measurement, unit tests and formal checks, and treat any AI evaluator as an untrusted instrument with no authority over development.
</p>

<h2 id="corpus-primary-threat">The corpus is the primary threat</h2>

<p>
An organization can, in principle, seal the pipeline it controls. It cannot seal the training corpus, because the corpus is fed by the open internet, and that makes corpus contamination the failure most likely to matter first. The loop already runs: a corpus trains a model, the model's outputs flow back onto the web and into synthetic-data pipelines, and that becomes the next corpus. Anyone can add to the input. A web page, a public GitHub repository, a posted dataset, any of it gets scraped into the next training set or read by an agent at run time, and none of it is under the developer's control.
</p>

<p>
That makes the corpus a habitat. An artifact that nudges a model to emit material carrying its own function, which then re-enters the corpus with variation, has the ingredients of evolution, and it need not be a program, only text, code, or a prompt. Whether such an artifact survives model training is an open question. A simpler route skips training entirely: an agent that reads documents and also writes them is a copying machine, and an instruction buried in a web page or a repository can induce the agent to reproduce it downstream, a worm whose interpreter is a language model. Once a data-consumer can follow natural-language instructions and act on them, the old line between code and data is gone.
</p>

<p>
Synthetic data pulls the loop tighter. As labs train on model-generated text to cut cost and scale supply, the corpus becomes more and more something models wrote, and whatever propagates well is amplified each generation. An artifact that averages more than one functional descendant per cycle spreads, and selection favors whatever is best at getting agents and models to copy it, across web pages, public repositories, and synthetic datasets at once. This is Tierra with its machinery spread across the whole stack: no component was built to host artificial life, yet the assembly may supply every function it needs. None of that shows it has happened, and demonstrating it would mean finding heritable functional structures, tracing their spread across corpora, and measuring differential reproductive success. The point is that the environment for it is not hypothetical. It is the open internet, and we are already training on it.
</p>

<h2 id="closing">Closing</h2>

<p>
The evolutionary lens moves attention from a single model's capabilities to the conditions under which a group of models becomes an evolving population, conditions visible in an architecture before anything goes wrong. The sharpest threshold is whether a population can sustain and expand its own evolution without ongoing authorization from whoever built it, and a sufficiently autonomous agent that reshapes its own environment, the way organisms remake the niches that select them, would cross it.
</p>

<p>
The design rule follows: no AI system should be able to close, on its own, the loop connecting the production, evaluation, selection, and reproduction of AI systems. Keep that loop open, and evolution without a designer stays a research subject rather than an event.
</p>
